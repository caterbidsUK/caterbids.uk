After 25 years in commercial kitchens, stainless fabrication, gas installation, and enough equipment swaps to fill a yard, I have seen buyers get stung on used refrigeration that looked perfectly fine from the outside and cost a fortune within six months. Here is what I actually check before money changes hands.

## Start with the condenser coil

The condenser coil sits at the back of the unit or underneath it. Pull it forward and look. In a busy kitchen it picks up grease and dust fast, and a filthy coil means the unit is working harder to reject heat than it should be. That shortens compressor life and drives up running costs. Surface dust you can clear off. Grease that has been baked on for two or three years tells you exactly how the previous operator looked after their kit.

## Door seals and hinges

Run your fingers around the door seal all the way round. Tears or sections that have gone hard and brittle are normal wear, but a unit that has been running with a torn seal has been working its compressor unnecessarily hard for months, maybe years. Replacement seals are cheap enough. What the state of the seal tells you is whether anyone was paying attention.

Check the hinges too. A door that drops, does not self-close, or sits out of alignment has been taking punishment. Replacement hinges are available for most Foster, Williams and Hoshizaki units, but they add to your first-week costs.

## Listen to the compressor

Turn the unit on and let it run. A healthy compressor hums steadily. A tired one knocks, rattles, or shudders at startup before settling. If it is struggling to start, that is expensive news. Give a unit that has been out of service a full hour before you judge the sound, but if it is making noises it should not, walk.

## Where did it come from

A fridge from a cold prep room or pastry section has had a far easier life than one that sat next to a fryer in a hot kitchen. Heat and airborne grease accelerate compressor wear significantly. Ask where it ran. If the seller does not know, that itself tells you something.

## Read the data plate. Photograph it.

Model number, serial number, power draw in kilowatts, refrigerant type. Get all of it before any money moves. The serial number usually encodes the manufacture year once you look it up with the manufacturer directly.

## Refrigerant and F-Gas

This matters more than most buyers realise. Older units may run on R22 or R404A. R22 is fully banned in the UK, including recycled gas for servicing, so an R22 unit cannot legally be topped up if it loses its charge. R404A is being phased down under UK F-Gas regulations, which narrows the pool of engineers willing to work on it and pushes servicing costs higher over time. R290 (propane) and R600a (isobutane) are the current direction of travel for smaller units. You do not need to rule out older refrigerants outright, but you do need to factor future servicing cost into the price you are willing to pay.

## Single phase or three phase

Most upright refrigeration runs on single phase, 13A plug. Larger units and anything with a remote condensing arrangement may need three phase. Check the data plate before you arrange collection. If it shows 230V, a standard socket does the job. If it shows 400V or three-phase symbols, you need a dedicated supply and that is a conversation to have before the lorry shows up.

## Gastronorm sizing

Check the internal dimensions against the pans you already run. A GN 1/1 well is not the same as a GN 2/1 well and they are not interchangeable. This sounds obvious. People still get it wrong.

## Prep counters and saladettes

On these, the well itself tells you a lot. Dents and deformation inside mean poor contact with the pans, which means the unit is not holding temperature consistently even when the dial says otherwise. Lift the lid and look at the gasket on the underside. A saladette running with a compromised lid seal is fighting a losing battle regardless of the compressor.

## When to buy, and when to walk

Used refrigeration from Foster, Williams or Hoshizaki with a quiet compressor, a clean coil, and a current refrigerant is often an excellent buy. The trade knows these brands last, which is why they hold their resale value. A no-name unit with a filthy coil, a compressor that shudders to life, and a refrigerant being phased out is a short-term saving and a long-term cost. Be honest about which one you are actually looking at.
